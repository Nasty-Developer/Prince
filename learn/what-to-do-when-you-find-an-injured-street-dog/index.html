Animal First Aid

What to do when you find an injured street dog

The first sixty minutes after an injury decide the outcome. Here is the sequence, the kit, and the mistakes that cause more harm than the accident.

7 min read · Reviewed guidance for India

Key points

  • Pressure for five full minutes stops more bleeding than anything else at home.
  • Carry the dog flat on a rigid surface if a vehicle was involved.
  • No human painkillers, ever.

Before you touch the dog

An injured dog is in pain, and a dog in pain bites without meaning to. Ask yourself whether you can do this alone. If the dog is large, panicking, or in the middle of a road, your first job is traffic control and a phone call, not handling.

If you can proceed, move slowly, speak quietly, and approach on the dog's side rather than from the front. Use a towel or a blanket as a barrier between your hands and the dog's mouth.

Control bleeding

Apply firm, direct pressure with a clean cloth or gauze for a full five minutes without lifting it to check. Lifting early restarts the bleeding.

If blood soaks through, add another layer on top rather than removing the first. A limb wound that will not stop bleeding needs pressure and a clinic, not a tourniquet from a video.

Do: Use a clean cloth, a towel or sanitary pads. Do not use cotton wool, which leaves fibres in the wound.

Stabilise before you transport

A dog hit by a vehicle may have internal injuries or a fractured spine. Slide it onto a rigid board, a tray or a firm cardboard sheet, keeping the spine as straight as possible, and carry it flat.

Do not offer food. Do not force water. Do not give any human medicine, including painkillers. Paracetamol and ibuprofen are toxic to dogs.

What actually goes in a rescue kit

A basic kit fits in a scooter box and costs very little:

  • Clean gauze, a roll of bandage and tape.
  • A small bottle of saline for flushing wounds.
  • A towel or blanket, and a rigid sheet for carrying.
  • A slip lead and a spare muzzle or a length of soft cloth.
  • Gloves, a torch, and a bottle of drinking water.
  • A phone with the nearest 24-hour vet saved in it.

Getting to a vet

Government veterinary hospitals, municipal animal dispensaries and charitable clinics in most Indian cities treat street animals at low or no cost; private clinics are faster. Call ahead and say what you are bringing, so they can prepare.

Record the time of the injury, what you saw, and what you did. The vet will ask.

After the clinic

Ask three questions before leaving: what to watch for, when to bring the dog back, and what pain relief is prescribed and for how long. Write it down.

Then find the dog's feeder. Aftercare that nobody continues for a week is not aftercare.

When to call a veterinarian

  • Any wound that is deep, dirty, or over a joint.
  • Suspected fracture, spinal injury or head injury.
  • Laboured breathing, pale gums, collapse, or blood in urine.
  • A dog that cannot stand within an hour of the injury.
